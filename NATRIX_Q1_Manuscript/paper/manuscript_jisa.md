# Introduction
A web application firewall sits on the request path, so every design choice is also a latency choice. Signature sets are interpretable and cheap, but they age quickly: a rule written for one framework version can miss the same vulnerability after a parameter rename, an encoding change, or a move to a JSON body. Learned detectors promise to close that gap, and the literature reports many high-accuracy results on public HTTP corpora. The deployment questions are different: which features were kept and why, which were dropped and on what evidence, who may promote a new rule or model, what stops one compromised or over-eager agent, and what exactly was measured when a paper claims a millisecond budget? The contribution of this paper is an auditable admission and promotion protocol plus an evaluation methodology, not a new detector.

Evaluation has the same problem. A detector that sees the request path can learn which endpoints are usually attacked. If the same endpoint appears in training and test, that prior is free. A random stratified split, which we call P1, therefore measures deployment-like performance on known endpoints, not generalisation to unseen ones. On CSIC 2010, a lexical full-request baseline reaches 96.57% accuracy under P1 but 69.53% under an endpoint-disjoint split (P2); its payload-only view, which never sees the path, stays at 96.03%. The full-request number is not wrong for a WAF that knows its endpoints; it is wrong as evidence of payload detection.

NATRIX makes three obligations explicit: feature admission, promotion authority, and protocol-sensitive evaluation. It is organised as nine roles with a narrow synchronous path and a wider governance layer. Only the five inline roles can affect the verdict, and only the Policy Agent can promote a candidate. The evaluation covers two components in depth, the feature-admission gate and CPPP; it does not evaluate the nine-role orchestration end to end, and we do not claim that the decomposition improves detection by itself. The contract is request-level, not payload-only: method, path, query, body, and user agent. The path is included because a WAF sees it and endpoint structure is real evidence; the view ablation and lexical payload view show what survives without it.

The paper has four parts. First, a protocol inversion: under P1 the lexical full-request baseline is stronger than NATRIX on all three corpora, but under P2 it collapses on CSIC while the payload view stays at 96.03%, and on SR-BH the payload view collapses while the path carries the signal. Second, the admission procedure: Gate A checks provenance, observability, determinism, and a frozen safety list; Gate B applies a frozen four-condition statistical rule; Gate C applies a measured extraction-cost budget. On CSIC 2010 it reduces a 22-dimensional contract to 20 dimensions, and a 24-candidate screen accepts 12 candidates statistically while admitting none because the accepted block costs 1.71 ms p95 against a 0.15 ms budget. Third, CPPP, a promotion protocol with quorum, canary, trust, and audit guards; the canary is evidence-coupled, so promotion requires N observations with zero benign hits; a `SeedTrust` action decouples trust from votes, and the protocol is model-checked, mutation-tested, and verified on exact-rational trust decay. Fourth, an evaluation protocol that reports every in-distribution result under P1 and P2, with lexical views, a generic control, cross-fitted stackers, a nested threshold check, a frozen CRS replay, a scale curve, a grouping sensitivity check, a dilution test, and a deployment audit that scores the shipped gateway as a canary and measures its enforcement boundary.

The results are mixed. Under a cross-fitted FPR <= 2% operating point, NATRIX reaches 95.55/1.93 on CSIC P1, 91.96/1.67 on ECML P1, and 90.79/1.96 on the SR-BH 20k sample; the lexical full-request baseline is stronger than NATRIX on every corpus at P1 (96.57/1.96, 93.93/0.43, and 95.41/1.98), an independent char-CNN is comparable on CSIC (96.58/1.02 payload), and the frozen CRS PL1 baseline is not competitive (66.24/0.03, 78.64/20.13, and 44.29/25.80). Under P2, CSIC NATRIX falls to 78.85/6.50, lexical full to 69.53/21.57, and lexical payload stays at 96.03/2.43; on SR-BH P2, NATRIX is 90.62/1.88, lexical full 94.75/2.01, and lexical payload 36.27/1.88.

> **Contributions.** An auditable feature-admission procedure with a measured cost gate; a model-checked promotion protocol with an evidence-coupled canary; a deployment audit of the shipped gateway against that protocol; a two-protocol evaluation that shows detector rankings invert between P1 and P2; and a reproducibility artifact with the evidence row for every admission and rejection.
>
> **Non-contributions.** Not a new classifier; not state of the art; the deployment audit is a fixed labelled probe suite, not shadow-mode or generalisation evidence; no adaptive-adversary evaluation; and CPPP is not implemented in the shipped proxy.

# Related work
Signature-based WAFs remain the operational default because they are explainable, cheap, and auditable at the rule level. ModSecurity with the OWASP Core Rule Set is the reference open implementation, and its paranoia levels expose the recall/false-positive trade-off directly [@ristic2010modsecurity; @owasp_crs]. The weakness is maintenance: a rule is tied to a syntax and a framework version, and an encoding change can evade it. Learned detectors replace rules with a model over request features, from n-gram and token models [@kruegel2003anomaly; @robertson2006behavior] to classical classifiers [@torrano2010anomaly; @sureda2022new; @garcia2009anomaly]. Reported numbers are often high, but protocols differ: some deduplicate, some do not; some split randomly, some by endpoint; some tune the threshold on the test set.

The bias that matters here is endpoint leakage. A request path is a strong prior: some endpoints are attacked more often than others, and a model that memorises the path can separate classes without learning the payload. Random stratified splitting keeps the same endpoint in training and test, so the prior is available at test time; group-disjoint splitting removes it. The same issue appears in malware and network intrusion detection, where duplicate or near-duplicate samples inflate random-split accuracy [@arp2022dos; @pendlebury2019tesseract], and drift explains why a fixed boundary degrades after deployment [@gama2014survey]. WCP 2024 makes the problem visible in an extreme form: every malicious request has path `/`, while legitimate traffic spans thousands of paths. The paper treats WCP as a diagnostic and uses the endpoint-matched subset for any payload claim.

Feature selection is grouped into filter, wrapper, and embedded methods [@guyon2003introduction]. The common weakness is that the selection is reported as a final set, not as a decision process. Pre-registration is the standard remedy in clinical and social science and is starting to appear in machine learning [@nosek2018preregistration]. The paper applies it at the feature level: the drop rule, safety list, candidate pool, and cost budget are frozen before the v4 outcome is inspected. The rule composes existing methods, with a written decision for every feature, plus the gate most feature-selection papers omit: a statistically useful feature that is too expensive to extract is not admitted. SHAP and permutation importance describe a fitted model; they do not justify a contract [@lundberg2017unified], and post-hoc explanation is not a substitute for an interpretable contract [@rudin2019stop].

Role-based security architectures are often described as a division of labour, but that is weak as a security argument because it does not say which agent may change policy. The paper makes the authority graph explicit and verifies the promotion path. Zero-trust principles motivate separating evidence producers from the policy owner [@nist2020zero].

**Positioning.** The individual components predate this paper; the contribution is the combination and the evidence standard: every decision has a recorded row, the cost gate is measured on the worst case, promotion is model-checked, and the ranking is reported under both protocols.

# NATRIX architecture and threat model
NATRIX is shaped by four constraints. The verdict is inline: a request must receive allow, block, or challenge before the application processes it, and the reference budget is p95 < 5 ms for the full path, not for inference alone. Evidence and authority are separate: the component that observes a signal is not necessarily the component that may act on it, and the ML Engine may score a request but may not promote a model or rule. Every promotion is auditable: a rule or model that becomes active must have a recorded origin, independent evidence, and a retirement path.

Figure 1 shows the architecture. **Telemetry Ingestion** parses the request, normalises method, path, query, headers, and body, computes the active feature vector, and attaches request identity and trace metadata; it does not decide. **Zero-Trust Verifier** checks identity and context (session state, route policy, authentication claims, request consistency), treats headers as claims rather than facts, and can veto or attach a confidence penalty. **Threat Intelligence** supplies independent evidence: signature matches, indicator lookups, unsafe-method detection, and known-bad user-agent patterns. **ML Engine** runs the binary gate, a Random Forest exported to ONNX (22 features in v3, 20 in the admitted v4 contract), plus the threat-typing model; it returns a probability and a family distribution and has no promotion authority. **Remediation Orchestrator** fuses the evidence, applies the active policy, and emits the verdict; it is the only inline role that can produce a block, and it does not train, promote, or mutate policy.

![Figure 1. NATRIX architecture. The synchronous path contains five roles and produces the per-request verdict. The asynchronous band contains four governance roles; only the Policy Agent may promote a candidate, and promotion requires quorum, canary evidence, trust, and an audit record.](figures/architecture.png){#fig:architecture width=4.9in}

The asynchronous band contains **Risk Scoring** (per-session and per-principal risk, which can raise a later request's risk but cannot retroactively change a verdict), **Adaptive Memory** (incident summaries, feature distributions, and drift indicators, off the decision path), **Policy Agent** (CPPP: owns the candidate lifecycle, is the only role allowed to promote or retire, and writes the audit record), and **Containment** (post-decision side effects such as session blocks, rule rollback, artifact quarantine, and operator notification). Containment is outside the inline path because it may involve external systems and cannot be bounded by the 5 ms reference. The paper evaluates the gate as the primary learned component; the orchestration logic is specified and unit-tested, and the headline accuracy numbers are gate numbers.

The contract is computed from the method, path, query, body, and user agent. It does not use response bodies, server logs, DNS, or external reputation. The path is included deliberately: a WAF sees it, and endpoint structure is real evidence. The paper therefore says *request contract*, not *payload contract*. The lexical baseline has a separate *payload view* that excludes the path; that view is a diagnostic, not a description of NATRIX.

The authority graph is acyclic with respect to promotion. Telemetry, Zero-Trust, Threat Intelligence, and the ML Engine may submit evidence. The Orchestrator may decide a request. Only the Policy Agent may change the active policy, and it may not fabricate evidence; it consumes votes and canary results produced by other roles. Containment may execute a policy decision but may not create one. CPPP makes each of those transitions impossible or explicit.

The adversary controls the HTTP request: method, path, query, headers, body, encoding, and user agent, and may attempt SQL injection, XSS, traversal, command injection, XXE, Log4Shell-style lookups, and obfuscation. The base threat model assumes that agent processes, model artifacts, and the audit log are not compromised, and that votes carry authenticated agent identity. CPPP addresses unauthorised promotion by a single agent and promotion without canary or quorum; it does not address Byzantine collusion among a majority of agents, Sybil identities, model poisoning, or supply-chain compromise. The evaluation is not an adaptive-evasion study: the corpora were not generated by an adversary who knows the feature set and threshold.

The current proxy implements the inline path and a simplified promotion path. A new signature is inserted with `hitCount = 1`; the next observation increments the count; the candidate becomes active when `hitCount >= 2` or the source contains `PCAP` or `Dataset`. There is no canary, independent quorum, trust value, or automatic decay. That is exactly the transition CPPP forbids, and the deployment audit below measures it on the live deployment. Replacing the shortcut is the deployment step; the formal results describe the specified protocol and its reference implementation, not the shipped proxy.

# CPPP and trust decay
CPPP is a finite-state protocol for candidate rules and models. A candidate has one of five states: observed, canary, promoted, retired, or rejected. The allowed transitions are: observed -> canary, when an independent quorum of at least $K$ agents has voted and trust is at least $\tau$; canary -> promoted, only by the Policy Agent, and only when the canary has passed, the quorum is still present, and trust is at least $\tau$; observed -> rejected, by explicit policy decision; and promoted -> retired, when trust falls below $\tau$ on a scheduler tick. The canary is an evidence event, not a free action: it requires at least $N$ observations, and none of them may be a benign hit. A hit is permanent, so a candidate cannot be rescued by later clean traffic. There is no observed -> promoted transition, no single-agent vote-and-promote path, and no promotion without an audit record. Quorum counts independent agent identities, not messages.

An earlier model coupled trust to votes: an agent gained trust by voting, and quorum was counted over agents already above the floor. The invariant was close to true by construction, and a mutant that removed the quorum guard could survive. The revision adds a `SeedTrust` action that sets trust independently of any vote. Votes are recorded separately, and the promotion guard checks both, so a mutant that removes the vote-count guard is killed even when trust is high. `SeedTrust` is an environment assumption, not a model of how trust is earned; it lets the model explore states in which an agent is trusted but has not voted, or has voted but is not trusted.

TLC checks nine safety invariants: `TypeOK`; `NoPromotionWithoutCanary`; `NoPromotionWithoutQuorum`; `NoPromotionWithoutTrust`; `NoPromotionWithoutObservations`; `NoPromotionWithCanaryHit`; `ActiveImpliesAudited`; `RetiredNotActive`; and `NoDirectPromotion`. The two observation invariants are the evidence coupling: a promoted rule must have at least $N$ observations and zero benign hits. These are safety, not liveness, properties. The model proves that a bad transition cannot occur, not that a good candidate will eventually be promoted.

Trust is a scalar in $[0,1]$. Without evidence it decays as $T(t) = T_0 e^{-\lambda t}$; positive evidence raises it towards 1 with gain $\alpha \in (0,1]$. The implementation uses an exact rational discrete model: one tick applies decay with factor $\rho \in (0,1)$, then reinforcement, so $\operatorname{tick}(T) = \rho T + \alpha(1-\rho T)$. Under stationary evidence the fixed point is $T^\ast = \alpha / (1-(1-\alpha)\rho)$ and the closed form is $T_n = T^\ast + (T_0 - T^\ast)((1-\alpha)\rho)^n$. The contraction factor is strictly below 1, so the iteration converges geometrically. A promoted rule whose trust falls below $\tau$ is retired atomically on the tick that would cross the floor; no observable state has a promoted rule below $\tau$.

The one-rule TLA+ configuration generates 939 states, 278 distinct, at depth 12; the two-rule configuration generates 444,245 states, 77,284 distinct, at depth 23. Both report no error, and the nine invariants hold. The executable reference is enumerated breadth-first to depth 9: 20,678 states and 97,870 transitions, with no violation, and 13 property tests pass. The mutation test removes one guard at a time; all eight mutants are killed (`no_quorum`, `no_canary`, `no_trust`, `no_audit`, `direct_promote`, `retired_active`, `canary_hit`, `no_observations`). Trust decay is checked on 17 trust points, four decay factors, four gains, and 200 steps: 81,484 cases, zero violations; nine symbolic identities are proved with SymPy. A sidecar over 916 audit records verified the hash chain, matched 108 promotions with 108 retirements (8 automatic), and held all eight runtime invariants. Promotion and retirement cost 5.15 and 4.76 ms p95 without fsync, and a full cycle 33.75 ms; fsync raises promotion and retirement to 53.57 and 48.04 ms. Validation is heavier (45.82 ms p95, 522.41 ms p99 under 32 parallel rules) and should be moved off the critical path.

# Feature admission
The runtime has four feature contracts: v1 (10-dimensional prototype), v2 (16-dimensional threat typing), v3 (the 22-dimensional request contract used by the binary gate), and v4 (the 20-dimensional contract produced by admission). They share extraction code and differ only in the mask; the ONNX export preserves input order. The 22 v3 features cover shape, lexical counts and maxima, evasion scores, signature scores, structural counts, and request-method risk. The names are historical: `payload_length`, `shannon_entropy`, and the ratio features are computed over the request target and body, not the body alone. Header-coherence features were deferred by a frozen decision (D1) because they would widen the contract beyond the fields the inline proxy currently passes.

## Feature origin

v1 (10 features) was the first prototype, assembled from indicators common in WAF rules and HTTP-anomaly work: request length, Shannon entropy, character-class ratios, token count, SQL/XSS/traversal keyword scores, and a scanner user-agent flag. v2 (16) added six features for the threat-typing model: command-injection patterns, encoding ratio, null-byte flag, duplicate-parameter count, HTML tag density, and request-method risk. v3 (22) added six lexical features for the binary gate: suspicious extensions, quote and angle counts, double escapes, and maximum parameter-value ratio and length. The seven safety features come from rules the runtime already enforced as direct policy signals. The v3 lexical block and the later candidate pool were shaped by error inspection of gate misses, but the v1-to-v3 growth was iterative and not frozen in advance; the admission procedure was introduced afterwards to audit the result, and this paper reports that sequence. The full formula, source field, and safety status of every feature are in `tables/feature_contract.csv` in the repository.

Admission is a decision procedure, not a search. A feature enters only if it passes three gates in order, frozen before the v4 outcome is inspected. **Gate A** requires the feature to be computable from the request fields the inline proxy already has, deterministic, bounded in cost, and independent of response bodies, logs, DNS, and external reputation. It must not be on the frozen safety list: seven features are retained because the runtime uses them as direct policy signals (`sql_keyword_score`, `xss_keyword_score`, `path_traversal_score`, `command_injection_score`, `null_byte_score`, `request_method_risk`, and `user_agent_anomaly`). Removing one from the model contract would not remove the policy rule; it would only make the audit misleading.

**Gate B** drops a feature only if all four conditions hold. **C1 (univariate):** the Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted $p$-value [@benjamini1995controlling] is at least 0.05 and the absolute Cliff's delta is below 0.10, or the feature is near-constant. **C2 (importance):** permutation importance is in the bottom quartile and its 95% bootstrap interval contains zero. **C3 (ablation):** leave-one-feature-out ablation shows no degradation larger than the practical-equivalence margin (0.2 percentage points of accuracy and 0.10 points of FPR). **C4 (safety):** the feature is not on the safety list. The conjunction is conservative: a feature weak univariately but important in the model is retained, and a statistically droppable safety signal is retained.

**Gate C** requires the feature to fit the measured extraction budget: added p95 cost on the 8 KiB worst case against a frozen 0.15 ms budget. A feature that fails Gate C is not admitted even if Gate B accepts it. The decision rule is $\operatorname{admit}(f) = \mathrm{Gate\,A}(f) \land \mathrm{Gate\,B}(f) \land \mathrm{Gate\,C}(f)$. A feature is *dropped* only if Gate A passes, Gate B fails, and it is not on the safety list. A feature that passes Gate B but fails Gate C is *not admitted* but is not said to be statistically useless; the distinction is recorded, and there is no post-hoc rescue.

Applied to the 22-dimensional v3 contract on CSIC 2010, the rule drops exactly two features: `param_pollution_count` and `html_tag_density`. Both are exactly zero in both classes, so C1 holds by the near-constant clause, and their importance and ablation are null. They are redundant dimensions, not signals removed because they were inconvenient. Both are constant on this corpus, so the evidence cannot show whether they carry signal elsewhere; they may. The cross-corpus audit is descriptive and does not re-run selection (D10). `token_count` is the conservative case: its permutation importance is -0.00054 with a 95% interval of [-0.00082, -0.00026], entirely below zero, so shuffling it slightly improves held-out performance, but C2 requires the interval to contain zero, so the feature is retained.

`special_char_ratio` is the more consequential anomaly. Its univariate association is strong (BH-adjusted $p \approx 10^{-180}$, Cliff's $\delta = 0.179$), and its permutation importance is positive (0.0831, rank 7 of 22), so C1 and C2 fail and the rule retains it. Yet leave-one-out ablation shows that removing it *improves* accuracy by 1.52 percentage points (95% CI [1.37, 1.68]) and lowers FPR by 0.24 points. C3 catches a harmful drop, not a beneficial removal, so it does not reject the feature. The direction matches the cross-corpus reversal: special-character density is higher in CSIC attacks but lower in ECML and SR-BH attacks, so the forest learns a corpus-specific direction. A post-hoc designer would drop it and report a 1.5-point gain; we do not, because that decision would follow the outcome. The 1.52 points are an honest bound on what the frozen rule leaves on the table.

The same mechanism screened 24 frozen candidates drawn from the literature, runtime capability, and error analysis. Twelve pass the statistical screen (mean delta F1 above its standard error and positive in at least four of five folds). None is admitted. Gate C is applied to the accepted block as a set, because the inline budget is an envelope for the additional extraction pass, not a per-feature allowance. The block costs 1.71 ms p95 on the 8 KiB worst case and 0.43 ms p95 on the real CSIC distribution, against the 0.15 ms envelope; the full 24-candidate pass costs 2.33 ms. Table 1 gives the per-candidate marginal cost and the set-level budget. Twenty candidates are individually below 0.15 ms. Even the nine accepted candidates that are individually below budget cost 0.73 ms as a set, because each shares a second parse.

The screen is not uniformly rejective. Table 2 is a positive control: the four v3-D path features pass Gate B and Gate C, are admitted, and change joint CSIC accuracy by +0.02 points. The procedure can admit a candidate when the evidence supports it; the 24-candidate outcome is a cost decision, not a prior against new features.

**How to add a feature.** The procedure is fixed before the candidate is scored.

1. Implement the feature in the Node and Python extractors with identical logic.
2. Gate A: use available request fields only; require deterministic, bounded cost.
3. Reject the feature if it is constant on the corpus or has |Spearman| >= 0.95 with an existing feature.
4. Add it to the 20-feature core; run the same five folds; accept only if the mean F1 gain exceeds its standard error and is positive in at least four of five folds.
5. Gate C: measure the whole accepted block's p95 cost on the 8 KiB worst case against the 0.15 ms budget.
6. Write the evidence row and the decision before looking at final test results.

# Evaluation protocol
Four labelled HTTP corpora are used. Table 3 summarises their composition, deduplication, and role. All are public or publicly redistributable research corpora. No DNS logs, incident text, unlabelled `http.log`, or production traffic is used for training or for any accuracy claim. Sources and checksums are recorded in `data/manifest.json`.

CSIC 2010 [@csic2010dataset] is the primary in-distribution benchmark. It is old and was generated against a small e-commerce application; it is useful because its endpoint structure makes the P1/P2 distinction visible, not because it represents modern traffic. ECML/PKDD 2007 [@raissi2007web] is the classic challenge corpus, older than CSIC and with a different label ontology; it is used as an in-distribution corpus and as a transfer target. SR-BH 2020 [@srbh2020dataset] is the modern corpus; its attack-dominant prior (299,508 attack vs 122,546 clean) is a property of the corpus, not a deployment. The full corpus is used for the scale analysis in Section 7.5; the shared 20,000-request sample is used wherever a baseline must be compared on identical rows. OpenAppSec WCP 2024 [@openappsec2024] supplies site-capped legitimate traffic and malicious payloads; every malicious request has path `/`, so a random split leaks the endpoint. WCP is never used for training or threshold selection; the endpoint-matched subset (`path == /`) is the diagnostic.

Duplicates are removed before splitting on (method, path, query, body, user agent). A key that appears with two different labels is dropped and counted.

**P1 (deployment-like)** is a stratified five-fold split over unique requests. No identical request crosses the split, but the same endpoint may appear in training and test. This is the headline protocol because it matches WAF operation: the endpoint is known, and the question is whether a request to it is malicious. **P2 (endpoint-disjoint)** is a stratified group five-fold split, where a group is defined by (method, normalised path, query keys, body keys) and assigned wholly to one fold. P2 is the generalisation bound, never the headline number. It is not path-free: the path string remains a feature, and what P2 removes is the identity of the endpoint as a memorisable training item. The P2 folds are built once per corpus and reused by every score-based detector, so the comparison is paired.

The primary learner is the shipped gate: a Random Forest [@breiman2001random] with 200 trees, unlimited depth, `min_samples_leaf=1`, `max_features=None`, no class weighting, and `random_state=42`. It is pinned to the deployed artifact (D5) and not tuned on test folds. The secondary learners are histogram gradient boosting and L2-regularised logistic regression on standardised features, both fixed untuned configurations (D9). All learners use scikit-learn [@pedregosa2011scikit], and the gate and typing models are exported to ONNX and served with ONNX Runtime [@onnxruntime2024].

The generic control is a 17-feature LightGBM over request-shape descriptors (method id, lengths, path depth, parameter counts, body-to-URL ratio, character ratios, entropy, longest token, mean value length, and has-query/has-body flags). It contains no SQL, XSS, traversal, or scanner keyword score and no NATRIX feature, and uses the same folds and 2% policy. The cross-fitted stackers combine out-of-fold scores with an L2 logistic model on logits; the stacker for fold $k$ is fitted on the other four folds and applied to fold $k$. Three stacks are reported: NATRIX+lexical, generic+lexical, and NATRIX+generic+lexical.

Three threshold policies are reported: F1-optimal, FPR <= 1%, and FPR <= 2%. The 2% policy is the deployment point because the acceptance target is FPR < 2.03%; it was added after the plan was frozen and is recorded as D8. The frozen protocol selects the threshold by inner five-fold cross-validation inside each outer training fold. That is the fully nested point, and it is conservative: inner models see 64% of the data while the scoring model sees 80%, so the probability scale differs. The cross-fitted calibration (D13) is a sensitivity check: for each outer fold $k$, the threshold is selected on the outer out-of-fold predictions of the other four folds and applied to fold $k$. Fold $k$'s labels are never used to select its threshold, but the models that score the other folds were trained on data that includes fold $k$, so this is a sensitivity analysis, not a fully nested selection. The headline comparison uses the cross-fitted 2% policy because every baseline can be calibrated the same way; the fully nested result is reported for the primary corpus as the stricter check.

**What "pre-registered" means here.** The analysis plan and candidate pool were frozen on 2026-10-30 in the project repository before the v4 screen and the matched-FPR comparison were run. This is a project-level freeze, not a third-party preregistration: no OSF record or public git history existed at freeze time. D8 (the 2% operating point) and D13 (cross-fitted calibration) were added after that freeze and are recorded as deviations in `protocol/deviations.md`; the headline 2% comparison is therefore a post-freeze analysis, not a confirmatory test. We use "frozen" in that limited sense and do not claim that the entire analysis was preregistered.

Accuracy, FPR, FNR, precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC, and PR-AUC are reported. Confidence intervals are percentile bootstrap intervals with 2,000 resamples [@efron1993introduction]. Paired comparisons use McNemar's test [@mcnemar1947note] and a paired bootstrap for the accuracy difference; because several learners and corpora are compared, we read individual tests as descriptive and do not apply a family-wise correction [@demsar2006statistical]. The feature audit uses Benjamini-Hochberg correction across the 22 tests. The three split seeds are 42, 123, and 2026; the model's `random_state` is fixed at 42 to match the shipped artifact.

The signature baseline is the official `owasp/modsecurity-crs` 4.25.2 nginx image at paranoia levels 1, 2, and 3, with the inbound anomaly threshold at 5, request-body inspection on, and response-body inspection off. The origin returns HTTP 200 for every request, so a 403 is the WAF decision. The replay sends the original method, path, query, body, user agent, and content type as HTTP; no NATRIX feature extraction is involved. Statuses 400, 405, 413, and 414 are nginx rejections that occur before ModSecurity evaluates the request and are reported as non-decisions. The full CSIC corpus is replayed at all three levels; ECML, SR-BH, and WCP at PL1. The primary table uses decisions only.

The lexical baseline is a hashed character 2-4 gram representation (`char_wb`, $2^{18}$ dimensions, L2-normalised) with an L2-regularised logistic-loss SGD classifier, fixed at `alpha=1e-5`, 30 epochs, and averaging. It is deliberately simple, single-pass, and untuned: a strong reference, not a new model. Three views are reported: `endpoint` (method and path), `payload` (query and body), and `full` (path, query, and body). The endpoint view tests whether a headline is an endpoint prior; the payload view tests whether the signal survives without the path. Thresholds are 0.5 and a leave-one-fold-out FPR <= 2% threshold, identical to the NATRIX policy, and the P1/P2 lexical rows use the same folds as NATRIX. An independent learned detector is also run: a small character-level CNN (32-dimensional byte embedding, two convolutional layers, max-pooling, five epochs) trained on the same folds and calibrated with the same policy. It is a self-contained reference, not a reproduction of a named system. Eight settings are reported (CSIC and SR-BH, P1 and P2, full and payload views); Table 4 includes them.

The latency protocol measures the full inline decision: request -> Node extraction -> HTTP `/predict` -> ONNX gate -> threat typing -> policy verdict. It uses the real Node proxy, Python service, and ONNX artifacts, so it includes JSON serialisation, not just inference. The input is 10,000 requests (5,000 CSIC, 3,000 ECML, 2,000 OpenAppSec); after 300 warm-up requests, 9,700 are measured, and failures are counted. The benchmark runs on one workstation (8 GB RAM, 12 logical processors) with a single-process Python service. It excludes synchronous SQLite writes, disk logging, and containment side effects, and is a component measurement, not a throughput test; the 5 ms line in Figure 5 is a reference point, not a pass/fail target.

## Cross-corpus transfer and direction reversal

Ten features reverse on both independent corpora. `hex_encoding_ratio` is +0.386 on CSIC but -0.537 on ECML and -0.417 on SR-BH; `special_char_ratio` is +0.179 on CSIC but -0.695 on ECML. A feature can be stable across two corpora and reverse on a third, so two corpora are not enough to establish universality.

The reversal is not a threshold problem: the direction of the feature-label relationship changes, so a model that learned a positive coefficient on CSIC will rank SR-BH attacks below clean requests on that feature. CSIC to ECML reaches 37.93% accuracy at 86.02% FPR; CSIC to OpenAppSec 39.43% at 88.80%; SR-BH to CSIC 63.06% at 21.72%. The ROC-AUCs tell the deeper story: CSIC to SR-BH is 0.435 and SR-BH to ECML is 0.486, both below chance. A below-chance AUC cannot be fixed by moving the threshold; the ranking itself is inverted. The remedies are local retraining, local feature selection, or a representation invariant across populations.

## Gate plus signature fusion

OR and AND fusion of the NATRIX gate and the frozen CRS replay treats CRS as the stand-in for the Threat Intelligence role. On CSIC, OR adds 14 attacks for 6 clean false positives: recall rises by 0.09 points and FPR by 0.03 points, and the accuracy change is not significant ($p=0.115$). The CRS true positives are almost a subset of the gate's, so the signature channel is redundant; AND is much worse, with recall falling to 26.75% because it is essentially the CRS decision.

On ECML, OR adds 1,483 attacks for 6,835 clean false positives (FPR 1.67% to 21.19%); on SR-BH, 653 for 1,463 (1.96% to 27.16%); on WCP, 22 for 860 (0.11% to 6.71%). At a 2% budget the signature channel either adds almost nothing or buys recall at three to twenty-five times the budget. This is a negative result for the gate-plus-threat-intelligence claim, and the paper reports it rather than presenting the OR recall gain in isolation.

## Score-level fusion

The negative CRS result does not rule out fusion with a complementary learned channel. A two-feature logistic stacker on the out-of-fold scores of NATRIX and the lexical baseline improves on the lexical baseline on CSIC (98.45% versus 96.57%, McNemar $p<0.001$), ECML (94.04% versus 93.93%, $p=0.0004$), and SR-BH (96.81% versus 95.41%, $p<0.001$). On WCP it does not: 99.95% versus 99.97%, $p=0.581$, because the corpus is already near ceiling for both detectors.

The CSIC gain survives a fully nested check: the outer five folds are held out, the base scores are produced by an inner four-fold procedure, and the stacker and threshold are fitted only inside the outer training folds. The nested result is 98.24% accuracy at 1.32% FPR with 97.72% recall, so the gain is not a threshold-selection artifact. The caveats matter. The lexical agent is not in the shipped proxy, so this is offline headroom. The threshold is still selected by cross-fitting, not by a fully nested procedure on every corpus. The gain is not universal: on CSIC P2 full the stack is worse than lexical alone, on CSIC P2 payload it is tied, and on SR-BH P2 payload the apparent gain is the NATRIX path signal re-entering the stack.

## WCP 2024 as a diagnostic

WCP is the newest corpus and the clearest example of a benchmark artifact. Every one of the 73,924 malicious requests has path `/`, while the 137,820 legitimate requests span thousands of paths from 692 sites. On the random 20k split, an endpoint-only lexical model reaches 100% recall at 1.31% FPR, and NATRIX reaches 99.79% accuracy.

Restricting to requests whose path is exactly `/` leaves 170 clean and 6,982 attack rows. NATRIX blocks 0 of the 170 clean rows (Wilson 95% upper bound 2.21%) and 99.64% of attacks. The lexical full and payload views reach 99.91% and 99.89% recall with 0 false positives; CRS reaches 88.80% recall at 24.12% FPR; the endpoint-only model collapses to 100% FPR, confirming that the random-split result was an endpoint artifact. The clean subset is small, the payloads are easy, and the legitimate traffic is site-clustered, so this is a diagnostic, not a generalisation claim.

# Formal verification

The formal claim is about control flow and trust arithmetic, not about whether the protocol chooses good rules, whether the canary sample is representative, or whether the agents are independent in a Byzantine sense. The TLA+ specification [@lamport2002specifying] `Cppp.tla` models three agents, a set of rules, a quorum $K=2$, a trust floor $\tau=2$, and a maximum trust of 3. The Python reference `cppp.py` mirrors the TLA+ guards, and the property suite `test_formal.py` contains 13 tests, all passing. The breadth-first enumeration is bounded at depth 9 and is not a proof for unbounded executions, but it exercises the implementation rather than only the specification. The mutation test removes one guard at a time and asks TLC whether the corresponding invariant is violated; all eight mutants are killed, which shows that the invariants are sensitive to the transitions they constrain. The trust-decay verification checks boundedness, monotone decay, the half-life definition, recovery bounds, the closed form, and convergence over 81,484 exact-rational cases; a floating-point grid would introduce tolerance decisions and could hide a violation near the boundary. The sidecar over 916 audit records verified the hash chain, matched 108 promotions with 108 retirements (8 automatic), and held all eight invariants; its full cycle cost 33.75 ms p95, with validation the operation to move off the critical path.

# Deployment audit and the enforcement boundary

CPPP is only useful if it can be applied to a real promotion decision, so we audited the existing deployment rather than building a new one. The gateway runs on Render, the SOC dashboard on Vercel, and the protected origin is a public portfolio reached through a path-scoped route. The audit has three parts: a repeated labelled probe suite, the gate's own decision ledger, and an offline replay of the predicate that actually enforces.

**Labelled probe suite.** Each run sends ten benign requests first (home page, campaign query, two SPA routes, static bundles, search, pagination, an apostrophe, an email parameter) and then ten attacks (SQL injection, reflected and evasive XSS, traversal, command injection, null-byte extension bypass, double encoding, scanner path, Log4Shell). Benign-first ordering prevents an attack-triggered adaptive block from contaminating the false-positive count. Three runs, 20 s apart, each returned TP=10, FP=0, TN=10, FN=0, and each rendered the origin's portfolio title; pooled over 60 probes, 30/30 attacks were blocked and 0/30 benign requests were blocked. This is a policy check on a fixed probe set, not a generalisation estimate.

**What the gate actually decides.** Only 33 of the 60 probes produced a gate decision. The other 27 were handled before the gate: two static bundles per run were served without a decision, and 21 attacks were blocked by the lexical rule layer. The gate ledger is nonetheless unambiguous. All 24 benign requests that reached it were scored as attacks, with probabilities from 0.75 to 1.00, and all 24 were marked inapplicable and allowed. All 9 attacks that reached it were scored above threshold and enforced. The gate is therefore not a calibrated detector on this traffic; it is a corroborator. Enforcement is the conjunction of the score with an independent lexical predicate (`gateHasLexicalSignal`): null byte, command injection, SQL, XSS, traversal, hexadecimal ratio above 0.2, or a suspicious extension. Inference on the audit rows took 0.23--0.45 ms (median 0.30 ms), so the model is cheap; the decision that produces the zero-FP result is the predicate, not the score.

**The cost of the conjunction.** Table 13 replays the predicate on the labelled corpora at the deployed threshold of 0.5. On CSIC P1 the model alone reaches 92.94% recall at 2.07% FPR; the conjunction gives 34.66% recall at 0.00% FPR. The same pattern holds under P2 (89.82/24.30 to 34.41/5.64), on ECML P1 (76.96/1.50 to 53.47/0.68), and on the SR-BH 20k sample (96.24/10.92 to 59.36/1.43). The predicate buys false-positive suppression by discarding most of the detector's recall. It is a deployment guard, not evidence that the model is calibrated, and it cannot be audited as a detector.

**Canary counterfactual.** A gate decision is a promotion decision in miniature. Treat the eight benign requests that reached the gate in one run as a canary sample for a candidate that fires whenever the score is at least 0.5. The candidate records eight observations and eight hits. In the evidence-coupled CPPP, `PassCanary` requires `canarySeen >= N` and `canaryHits = 0`, and `NoPromotionWithCanaryHit` makes a hit permanent; the rule is refused. The shipped proxy instead inserts a learned signature with `hitCount = 1` and activates it when `hitCount >= 2`, with no benign-evidence test. The audit therefore exhibits on live traffic the exact shortcut that the protocol forbids, rather than only in the model. The counterfactual does not show that CPPP would improve accuracy; it shows that CPPP refuses a promotion that the operator cannot justify with benign evidence.

**Scope.** The deployment is one public gateway and one origin, the probe set is small and hand-written, and the canary sample is not representative. The audit shows that the deployment path works and that the enforcement boundary is real; it is not a shadow-mode trial, a generalisation estimate, or a claim that the gate is calibrated. A later restart reported the model as unavailable while the rule layer still blocked the attacks, so availability is a separate operational risk. The per-run files, pooled aggregate, and raw gate ledger are in `experiments/results/live_gate/`, together with the 20 probe definitions.

# Discussion

The evidence supports six claims.

First, the three-gate mechanism is a reusable decision procedure, not a trial-and-error search. It drops two features, retains 20, and produces a written justification for every row; it screens 24 candidates, accepts 12 statistically, and admits none on measured cost. Second, the inline path has a measured p95 of 3.78 ms over 9,700 real requests, and the HTTP hop, not the model, dominates; the cost gate separates marginal extraction cost from the shared parse and transport. Third, CPPP's control-flow safety is model-checked, mutation-tested, and implemented as a reference implementation: `SeedTrust` removes the construction artifact, eight of eight mutants are killed, and the protocol forbids the direct promotion shortcut that the shipped proxy still uses.

Fourth, the comparison protocol is itself a contribution. NATRIX is competitive with a strong lexical baseline under P1 but does not beat it; under P2 the ranking changes, with lexical full collapsing on CSIC while lexical payload stays strong, and the opposite pattern on SR-BH. Fifth, the control stack shows that the curated contract carries signal beyond a generic request-shape model, but fusion gains are protocol- and view-dependent, and the SR-BH P2 payload "gain" is the path signal re-entering the stack. Sixth, the dilution fix is a measured partial improvement: v3-D raises clean and mutated recall and reduces the targeted loss from 33.32 to 28.44 points, but introduces new weaknesses on case changes and SQL-comment insertion.

The evidence does not support universal generalisation. ECML reaches 91.96% under P1 and misses the accuracy target, and P2 fails on CSIC (78.85% at 6.50% FPR) and on the 20k SR-BH sample relative to the full corpus. Transfer is worse: two AUCs are below chance, and the OpenAppSec false-positive rate is 88.80% with a CSIC source. Ten features reverse sign, so the learned boundary is population-specific. The evidence also does not support a claim that NATRIX is state of the art, that v4 improves accuracy, that the mutation test establishes adversarial robustness, that gate-plus-CRS fusion is useful at a 2% budget, or that the shipped runtime enforces CPPP.

The inline/async split is worth preserving: the five inline roles account for the measured budget, while the four governance roles do not affect the current verdict. Feature direction is not portable, so cross-deployment federation needs explicit heterogeneity handling [@mcmahan2017communication; @kairouz2021advances]. The HTTP hop dominates latency, so a hard bound needs a persistent connection, co-located inference, or a Unix socket.

# Limitations
**Corpus age and construction.** CSIC 2010 and ECML/PKDD 2007 are old and were generated against specific applications; their traffic does not represent modern APIs, JSON, or cloud-native deployments. SR-BH 2020 is more modern but attack-dominant, so its prior is not a deployment prior. WCP 2024 is the newest corpus, but its legitimate traffic is capped at 200 requests per site and is site-clustered, and every malicious row has path `/`; only the endpoint-matched subset is reported. The corpora use different label ontologies, so a request labelled attack in one corpus may be clean in another; deduplication removes exact conflicts but not semantic ones.

**Split protocol and threshold selection.** P1 allows the same endpoint in training and test. That is realistic for a WAF but inflates performance relative to an unseen-endpoint deployment. P2 is the bound, and it fails on CSIC; it removes endpoint identity but not the path string, so it is not a payload-only protocol. The fully nested threshold policy misses the 95% target on CSIC by 0.33 to 0.41 points. The cross-fitted protocol meets it, but the models that score the other folds were trained on data that includes the held-out fold, so it is not fully nested. The paper reports both and does not present the cross-fitted number as frozen.

**Learner and screening configuration.** The forest is pinned to the shipped configuration (D5), which is not tuned for these corpora. A tuned forest might improve accuracy but would no longer be the deployed artifact. The secondary learners are fixed untuned robustness checks, not a model search. A single LightGBM configuration is the generic control; a stronger control would change the comparison.

**Formal scope.** TLC checks finite constants (three agents, two rules, $K=2$, $\tau=2$, max trust 3), and the BFS is bounded at depth 9. The model does not cover Byzantine agents, Sybil identities, model poisoning, or supply-chain compromise, and the trust values are not calibrated to real agent reliability; it is a safety layer, not a complete security proof.

**Latency and adversarial scope.** The benchmark is a single-workstation component measurement. It excludes synchronous SQLite writes, disk logging, and containment side effects, uses one Python and one Node process, and does not measure concurrency, a load balancer, or TLS termination. The p95 is a component result, not a service-level guarantee. The mutation study applies a fixed, bounded set of transformations, not an adaptive search; a mutated payload is not guaranteed to remain exploitable, and no adversary probes the detector for evasions [@biggio2018wild].

**Baseline, fusion, and scale comparability.** CRS is a frozen PL1-PL3 configuration, not a detector tuned to a 2% budget, so its rows are not like-for-like. The lexical baseline is a standard hashed n-gram learner and the char-CNN a self-contained reference; neither reproduces a named system. The NATRIX+lexical stacker is offline: the lexical agent is not in the shipped proxy, and a deployed stacker needs both scores at request time. Only the CSIC result is repeated under a fully nested protocol.

# Conclusion
The paper's contribution is the evidence standard, not a new detector. The results are two-sided. On the positive side, the inline p95 is 3.78 ms over 9,700 real requests, the three-gate mechanism drops two features and admits none of the 12 statistically accepted candidates on measured cost, CPPP's invariants hold in TLA+ and in the executable reference, eight of eight mutants are killed, trust decay is verified on 81,484 exact-rational cases and nine symbolic identities, the deployed gateway blocked 30/30 probe attacks with zero benign blocks, and the NATRIX+lexical stacker improves on the lexical base on CSIC P1, ECML P2, and SR-BH P2 full. On the negative side, NATRIX is not the best single detector: the lexical full-request baseline is stronger than NATRIX at the 2% FPR point under P1 on all three corpora, the char-CNN is comparable on CSIC, lexical payload is the strongest CSIC P2 detector, the fully nested policy reaches 94.67% and misses the 95% target, P2 fails on CSIC (78.85% at 6.50% FPR), two transfer AUCs are below chance, ten features reverse direction, gate-plus-CRS fusion is redundant or over budget, bounded mutation removes 10.13 points of recall, and the targeted dilution attack still removes 28.44 points with v3-D. v4 is not claimed to be more accurate or meaningfully faster; its value is that every feature's presence or removal is justified by recorded evidence, and WCP is reported only as an endpoint-confounded diagnostic.

The conclusion is not that the detector is universal or state of the art, but that the architecture and the evidence standard are reusable. A deployment should calibrate on local data, evaluate on endpoint-disjoint splits, report endpoint-only and payload-only baselines, monitor feature directions for reversal, and treat promotion as a protocol with quorum, canary, trust, and audit. The current runtime does not yet enforce CPPP; replacing the `hitCount >= 2` and import-shortcut path is the next engineering step. Future work has five directions: shadow-mode validation with a labelled window and rollback; adaptive adversarial evaluation [@biggio2018wild]; federated learning with local heads [@mcmahan2017communication; @kairouz2021advances]; header-coherence features, deferred by D1; and a dilution-invariant contract using per-parameter maxima, with a new cost-gate measurement.
